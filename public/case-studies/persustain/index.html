Summary

Persustain began with a compelling five-year business vision connecting individual climate action to shared projects, future verification, and eventually carbon-market participation. But it existed only as an investor deck, not yet as a product.

The fastest way to deliver a four-month MVP was another carbon tracker. But that would have reduced the client's vision to its smallest, least distinctive part. Instead, I repositioned tracking as the entry point into a broader participation system, one designed to turn individual actions into shared, eventually verifiable impact.

Over four months, I led product strategy and UX from concept to a working MVP, translating that investor deck into a product designed for users to understand, engineers to build, stakeholders to demonstrate, and the team to continue developing in Phase 2.

01

The Strategic Pivot

Deciding what kind of product this needed to be.

Joining Persustain at the zero-to-one stage, I inherited an investor pitch deck with no existing product or dedicated team. I selected and assembled a cross-functional team from within the agency, balancing the expertise the project required with available capacity, then led product design and delivery to turn the five-year vision into a working MVP.

The client had a compelling five-year strategy that connected individual climate actions to shared environmental projects, third-party verification, and future carbon-market participation.

There were no user journeys, information architecture, feature priorities, or shared product architecture. Even the most fundamental question remained unanswered: what would someone actually do inside the app?

As Lead Product Designer, I led Phase 1 product and design decisions, defining the MVP scope, feature set, Information Architecture, and UX/UI direction. Early on, I partnered closely with the engineering team to evaluate technical feasibility and align on the tech stack, ensuring our design solutions were viable from day one while they owned system architecture and implementation.

We were also working against a hard constraint: the MVP needed to be designed and delivered within four months for an incubator milestone review, leaving little room for experimentation or major course corrections.

The challenge wasn't simply designing an interface. It was deciding what kind of product this needed to be before a single screen could be justified.

The Decision That Changed the Project

I needed to answer a more fundamental question: were we building the right product?

If the goal had simply been to deliver an MVP on time, building a simple carbon tracker would have been the obvious choice. It fit the timeline, reduced technical complexity, and followed a familiar pattern used across the industry. But the client's long-term vision wasn't another tracking app. Its differentiation was a shared contribution system where individual actions could eventually become verified collective impact. If we reduced the MVP to tracking alone, we risked delivering a product that was easy to build but failed to demonstrate what made the business unique. I needed to understand whether the client's vision addressed a genuine gap in the market before making it the foundation of the MVP.

Finding the Real Problem

To answer that question without the budget or timeline for a dedicated research phase, I ran a rapid competitive analysis across five climate apps (Klima, Commons, Earth Hero, LiveGreen, and JouleBug), reviewing more than 80 public user reviews alongside each product's onboarding, interaction patterns, and retention strategies.

I was trying to understand why people stopped using these products after the initial excitement wore off, not looking for interface inspiration. Because this was secondary research rather than validation with Persustain users, I treated the findings as directional evidence for defining the MVP hypothesis.

The Insight

The research strengthened the case for a hypothesis the investor deck had only hinted at. The real opportunity wasn't building a better carbon tracker. It was using personal tracking as the entry point into a much larger participation system.

Rather than treating tracking as the destination, I reframed it as the beginning of a broader user journey, one that connected individual behaviour to shared climate projects, future verification, visible collective impact and potential future value.

Suddenly, the client's long-term business vision wasn't competing with the MVP. Instead, it became a product hypothesis worth testing, one that directly responded to the behavioural gaps we had identified.

The strategic shift

This changed the product question completely.

From

How do we build a better carbon tracker?

To

How might we help people see their individual actions as part of a larger collective outcome?

That single shift became the foundation for every design decision that followed.

02

Defining the Product System

Turning a hypothesis into something engineers could build.

The research gave me enough directional evidence to define an MVP hypothesis. The next challenge was turning that hypothesis into a product engineers could build.

Before designing interfaces, I needed to define the system itself. The investor deck described concepts like community projects, verification, certificates, and carbon value, but none of them existed as product entities yet. Without a shared understanding of how those pieces related to one another, design and engineering risked solving different problems. So I focused on creating the product foundation that every design and technical decision could build upon.

Building a Shared Product Language

I planned and facilitated an OOUX (Object-Oriented UX) workshop with stakeholders (developers and the client) to identify the platform's core objects and their relationships.

Rather than treating the workshop as a design exercise, I used it to establish a shared product language across business, design and engineering. Together, we defined the core objects, their relationships and the lifecycle connecting them. I then synthesised those decisions into the canonical object map that underpinned the user journeys, functional specifications and implementation discussions that followed. This gave the team a stable product architecture before implementation began, reducing the risk that each discipline would interpret the investor vision differently.

Modelling the Whole Lifecycle

With the core objects established, I defined the complete six-stage lifecycle of a contribution.

Built in Phase 1 Actions Aggregation Simulated in Phase 1 Verification Registry Certification Value

I modelled these as connected stages rather than independent future features. Activities could contribute to a shared project during an aggregation period, represented through evidence progress and explicit lifecycle states.

The MVP implemented the internal objects and states needed to represent this lifecycle. However, external verification bodies, registry connectivity, certification and market issuance remained simulated. This distinction gave Phase 2 a defined starting point without presenting external infrastructure as operational.

Structuring the Experience

With the product architecture established, the next challenge was making a technically complex system understandable without reducing it to a simplistic tracker.

I translated the product architecture into an information architecture centred on the journey from individual action to verified collective impact. Rather than exposing technical components such as evidence packs, registries and verification workflows, I organised the experience around two primary destinations: the Dashboard and the Project page.

The Dashboard was designed to answer the user's first question: did my actions matter? It does more than report a footprint. Alongside a personal emissions summary, My Impact So Far shows attributed contribution, contribution share, estimated value, and the progress of the project that contribution supports. Rather than reporting emissions alone, the design connects personal activity to the shared project it supports.

Selecting the project was designed to answer the next question: where did my contribution go? Rather than sending users through multiple screens, the Project page explains the entire system in one place. It connects an individual's contribution to overall project impact, exposes the complete impact lifecycle, and shows completed and in-progress lifecycle steps while labelling uncertain or future information as Estimated, Pending or Simulated. Methodology and assumptions remain visible alongside those states, allowing users to understand both how the system works and what has, or hasn't, been verified. Together, these destinations created a progressive path from personal activity to shared, eventually verifiable impact.

03

Designing for Trust

Showing a five-year vision without claiming it was already live.

By this point, the product communicated how individual actions became collective impact. The remaining challenge was trust.

The client's long-term lifecycle connected individual actions to aggregation, third-party verification, registry participation, certification and value creation. Removing those workflows from the product would have stripped away its core differentiation. Pretending they already existed would have undermined the trust we were trying to build. Neither option was acceptable. I needed a way to communicate the complete product vision while remaining completely honest about what had, and hadn't, been built.

Making Product Maturity Visible

Before high-fidelity design began, I specified two dimensions the interface needed to communicate: lifecycle progress and information certainty. I designed a distinct system for each.

This distinction allowed the product to communicate its long-term direction without presenting verification, registry integration or carbon-market participation as completed functionality.

Transparency as a Product Principle

The transparency approach extended beyond status labels. Methodologies, calculation assumptions, verification progress and lifecycle status were available at relevant points throughout the experience. The interface exposed not only the information itself, but also how it had been produced and how much certainty the product could reasonably attach to it. Rather than asking people to trust the platform, the interface provided the context they needed to make that judgement themselves.

Keeping AI Interpretive, Not Authoritative

Working with the AI developer, I defined boundaries for how generated content could appear within the product. OpenAI supported plain-language insights and evidence-pack summaries using the product's structured data.

AI could not calculate or verify project impact, issue credits, certify outcomes or determine market legitimacy. Those outcomes depended on calculation sources, supporting evidence, external review and later lifecycle stages.

These guardrails allowed AI to support explanation without letting generated content imply authority the product had not earned.

Balancing MVP Constraints with Long-Term Vision

This approach to trust and transparency balanced product honesty with the need to communicate the long-term vision. The interface separated lifecycle status from trust-state labels, although whether users understand those distinctions or whether they reduce confusion or increase trust, remains a Phase 2 research question. For the business, the MVP made the broader product journey demonstrable to investors and incubator stakeholders without presenting future capabilities as already operational.

Looking back, one of the most important product decisions on the project

Trust isn't built by hiding uncertainty, it's built by making uncertainty understandable.

04

Leading Cross-Functional Delivery

Reconciling business goals, user needs and technical constraints.

The shared product architecture became most valuable during implementation, when business goals, user needs and technical constraints had to be reconciled continuously.

I held primary responsibility for MVP scope, product logic, UX direction and how features expressed the agreed object model and lifecycle. The Technical Lead guided technical architecture and feasibility; the Junior Design Engineer produced the detailed interface and implemented the frontend and Firestore flows; and the Junior AI Developer implemented AI behaviour and integrations. The client provided the long-term business vision, domain context and feedback, making their alignment and confidence essential throughout the engagement.

When trade-offs surfaced across those responsibilities, I facilitated decisions by tracing the question back to the relevant object, relationship or lifecycle state before debating an interface solution. The activity-log example below shows how that approach worked in practice.

Supporting Implementation

Delivering the product required continuous collaboration with engineering, not a single hand-off. We held weekly cross-functional meetings alongside asynchronous updates, with focused working sessions whenever a decision required more than a status check.

I translated product decisions into detailed user journeys, interaction flows and functional specifications, then remained involved throughout implementation, answering questions, adapting designs when technical constraints surfaced and reviewing completed flows with engineering. Our internal checks covered core functionality, data flows and lifecycle progression.

One design review exposed a significant usability risk in the activity log. An early version required users to enter technical consumption values, but most people would not know how much electricity their kettle used when boiling water or which emissions factor applied. Asking for information users could not reasonably provide would make logging slower and reduce confidence in the resulting estimate.

I initially explored allowing users to describe an activity in natural language, for example, "boiled water with an electric kettle", and having the system interpret it and calculate the carbon-footprint estimate automatically. Engineering determined that reliably translating unrestricted text into all the inputs required for calculation was not feasible within the MVP timeline. Climatiq could calculate an emissions estimate once it received structured data; the difficult part was accurately inferring that data from an open-ended description.

We agreed on a simpler compromise: a searchable catalogue of familiar activities. OpenAI could enrich a description or help match it to a likely activity, but it did not calculate the carbon footprint or autonomously determine every required input. Users confirmed the activity and entered only a quantity they could reasonably know, such as frequency, duration, distance or amount. The resulting structured data was passed to Climatiq, which produced the emissions estimate. The result was presented as Estimated, with the relevant methodology and assumptions available for review.

The solution was less flexible than unrestricted natural-language logging, but it could preserve the core usability goal while remaining technically feasible and transparent about the certainty of its calculations. This became a pattern throughout implementation: we evaluated technical trade-offs against the underlying user need rather than defending a particular design solution.

Guiding MVP Scope

The client's vision included numerous opportunities for expansion: gamification, personalised sustainability plans, rewards, advanced analytics and social features. Many had genuine value. The harder question was not whether an idea was good, but whether it was right for this stage.

With a four-month deadline, the goal was not to maximise the feature list. It was to deliver one complete experience that made the relationship between individual action and collective impact clear enough to evaluate.

The scope test I introduced

Does this strengthen the core behavioural loop, or make it harder to assess on its own?

The clearest test came when the client wanted to introduce rewards and gamification during Phase 1. Rather than rejecting the idea outright, I brought the conversation back to the hypothesis we had agreed to examine first: whether the individual-to-collective loop was compelling without additional engagement mechanics.

We agreed to defer rewards, gamification and personalisation until after usability testing. Because the decision was evaluated against an agreed product question (not personal preference) the client supported it. This kept Phase 1 focused on a complete individual-to-collective experience and created a clearer hypothesis for Phase 2 testing.

Closing the Design-to-Build Gap

Once I established the core UX artifacts (user flows, user journeys) and the lean design system, I delegated screen design and UI production in Figma to the Junior Design Engineer. Upon my review and approval, she then built those screens directly in FlutterFlow, while I maintained oversight of critical flows and implementation specs. I guided her through regular critiques that evaluated accessibility, usability, visual design fidelity, and whether the build faithfully reflected our agreed object model and lifecycle logic.

That alignment wasn't a one-time result of the workshop. During one review, an early UI pass had drifted back toward a conventional carbon tracker and omitted the shared-project logic that made Persustain distinctive. I traced the issue back to the agreed object and lifecycle models, using the shared language, not my opinion, showing which relationships had disappeared from the interface. We then revised the experience so that personal tracking visibly connected to shared projects and lifecycle progress.

05

Outcomes & What's Next

What Phase 1 established.

Phase 1 was delivered within the agreed four-month scope for the incubator milestone review. The working MVP demonstrated the relationship between individual action, shared projects and the future verification lifecycle, turning the client's investor deck into something stakeholders could demonstrate rather than a vision they could only describe.

The client funded a Phase 2 engagement and continued with us. This is meaningful evidence of delivery and client confidence, but it is not evidence of user adoption or market impact.

Phase 1 also established shared object, lifecycle and trust-state models, supported by documented user flows and functional specifications. These foundations give Phase 2 a defined conceptual starting point. Whether they reduce future rework or improve delivery speed has not yet been measured.

Persustain was delivered as an incubator MVP rather than a public launch, so there are no post-launch activation, retention or engagement metrics to report. The same principle that governs the product's trust-state design governs this case study: nothing is presented as measured until it has actually been measured.

What I'll Validate First in Phase 2

Phase 2 extends beyond usability testing. It includes continued product development toward the later lifecycle stages (verification, registry participation, certification and value creation) while testing the core experience with real users.

My first product-design priority will be to test the assumptions Phase 1 left unproven:

  • Q01 ?

    Whether users understand how their activity contributes to a shared project

    Comprehension

  • Q02 ?

    Whether Estimated, Pending and Simulated increase trust rather than create confusion

    Trust labels

  • Q03 ?

    Whether the Project page gives people a stronger reason to return than personal tracking alone

    Retention

The findings will inform how we refine the core loop and introduce later lifecycle capabilities. After launch, I will establish behavioural baselines before setting targets for activation, repeat activity logging, project-page engagement and return behaviour.

Once those questions are better understood, I will also test how users respond when a project is delayed or unsuccessful, whether transparent communication maintains trust or causes them to lose confidence in the platform.

06

Reflection

An investor deck and a product spec are written in two different languages.

If I'm honest, the moment that's stayed with me isn't a screen, it's realizing that an investor deck and a product spec are written in two different languages. Most of my early value came before interface design: translating an ambitious business narrative into objects, relationships, lifecycle rules and product decisions the team could build.

Persustain's business model was already coherent to the people building it into a five-year narrative. What it wasn't, yet, was buildable.

The harder lesson came later: defining a system doesn't mean it survives execution. Even with a documented object model, someone still has to notice when execution quietly slides back toward the familiar, and use the shared language, not authority, to correct it.

And the trust-state pattern (Estimated, Pending, Simulated) is the piece I'm proudest of, and the one I'm least certain about. I designed it to solve an honesty problem: how do you show a five-year vision without lying about what's live today. Whether those distinctions genuinely increase user trust is a question only real users can answer, and that's exactly what Phase 2 is intended to validate.

Product design isn't always about removing complexity. Sometimes the harder work is organizing it so people can understand what is true, what comes next, and what they can confidently do. The interface was one expression of that work; the more meaningful contribution was creating a shared understanding across business, design, and engineering that turned an ambitious climate strategy into something buildable.
